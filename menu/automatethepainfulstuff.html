Introduction

“You just replaced two months of Julys in that crawlspace.” A friend had spent the previous three summers dragging a shop-vac through a 92°F attic to chase a mildew smell down to its source. The fourth summer, a tethered rover on a spool of Cat6 mapped every truss bay with a $40 gas sensor and a thermal camera, found the leak in an afternoon, and never asked to be paid in Gatorade.

This is the counterpart to Automate the Boring Stuff with Python. Boring work is the apprenticeship of physical skill and should stay human; painful work — that which damages the human form faster than it can heal — is what we delegate to machines. The chapters that follow enumerate those tasks, and the mechanisms an engineer can build against them.

Who Is This Book For?

This book is written for structural, mechanical, electrical, and robotic engineers who want a defensible answer to what to build next. It is not a survey of the state of the art, nor a catalog of commercial platforms. It is a decision procedure and twenty-six worked examples.

If you have ever finished a project and wondered whether it was worth automating in the first place, this book is for you. If you have ever built the wrong machine because the task looked tractable rather than valuable, this book is for you.

The Decision Rule

For any candidate task, estimate the rate of damage accumulated per exposure, d(damage)/dt; the rate of adaptation gained per exposure, d(adaptation)/dt; the recovery time required, Tr; and the interval between work cycles, Ti. Automate when damage outruns adaptation, or when recovery outruns the interval. Assign the human when the reverse holds — each cycle then yields net resilience, and the work becomes training stimulus. Where neither obtains, redesign the task before building the robot.

A Note on Scope The chapters are ordered by priority, not by difficulty. Part I — the envelope of the workspace itself — comes first because it defends the substrate on which every other kind of work is done. The robot is the immune system of the workspace, not a servant performing chores.
